The bar

Complete enough for what?

There's no single bar for a complete brief. There's a bar per commercial model, and they're a long way apart.

Two kinds of thing go missing from an inquiry, and they don't behave the same.

The bar doesn't move
Expectations

Budget. Who decides. What success means. The hard constraints. The problem underneath the request. The bar here doesn't move. A brief vague about who signs off is a bad brief whether you're selling discovery or a fixed-price build.

The bar moves with the risk
Specification

What actually gets built. The bar here moves with who's carrying the risk. "A checkout, some kind of loyalty thing" is enough to staff a team and start work on time and materials. It's nowhere near enough to promise a number, because on fixed price every fuzzy edge is money you've already committed.

Same rubric either way. Same ideal answers. What changes is how close to them you need to get.

So Firmground doesn't ask what you're hoping to sell. The material decides that. Under 60, sell discovery first. 60 to 79, time and materials. 80 and up, you can commit to a price. Your bands, in firm settings, applied the same way every time.

If you want to bid fixed price and this one came back at 45, that's not a verdict. It's a list of work to do, and how much of it you do is your call.

05
The standard

What's a rubric?

Your leadership writes this once. Everyone else never has to think about it.

A rubric is your firm's definition of a complete brief for one kind of work: what has to be known before anyone can responsibly put a number on it. Each criterion has two parts.

A description. What you need to know, and why it changes the price.

An ideal answer. What a good answer looks like, in your words. Not a form for the client to fill in. A reference point, so Firmground can tell the difference between a brief that mentions success metrics and one that actually has them.

One rubric per service you sell. That does two jobs. A discovery brief and a build brief need different things known, so the questions come out right. And taken together, your rubrics are a written description of what your firm does, which is what lets Firmground tell you when an inquiry falls outside it.

Start from one of ours and edit it. Change it whenever you learn something. Past scores stay on the version they were scored against.

The output

And every brief comes out the same shape.

One inquiry arrives as a spreadsheet. The next is a slide deck. The one after that is a call transcript and a forwarded thread. Your team reads each one cold, in whatever order the client happened to write it.

What comes out of Firmground always looks the same. Same criteria, same order, what the client said and what's been assumed marked the same way, every time.

After ten of these, your team stops estimating from scratch. This one looks like the retail build from last spring, and you know how that one went. That isn't a time saving. It's something your firm couldn't do at all before, because no two briefs were ever comparable.
